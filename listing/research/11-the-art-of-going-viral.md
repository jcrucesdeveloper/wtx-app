# The Art of Going Viral: Funny, Not a Commercial (2026)

Researched 2026-10-06 after batches A and B were judged, correctly, "boring,
they look like a commercial, people will skip them". Question: what actually
gets shared, what is funny, and how does a brand post it without it smelling
like an ad? About 28 pages read. Vendor pages are direction only; the academic
findings are marked.

## What actually spreads

1. **High-arousal emotion, and amusement is one** (academic). Berger & Milkman
   analysed ~7,000 New York Times articles: content that evokes high-arousal
   emotion (awe, amusement, anger, anxiety) is shared; low-arousal (sadness,
   mild contentment) is not. Guadagno et al.: videos that evoke amusement are
   significantly more likely to be shared. "Nice" and "informative" are low
   arousal. Batches A and B are nice.
2. **People share to say something about themselves and to each other**
   (academic + practitioners). Sharing projects identity ("social currency");
   a meme is an in-joke that marks who belongs. "This is so me" → like/save;
   **"this is so you" → a DM**. Instagram ranks on **sends** (Mosseri; Doing
   Things, 40+ accounts, rebuilt their whole strategy around "would you forward
   this?"). The best prompt they use: *"what's your biggest pet peeve?"*
3. **Low effort to consume**: instantly understood, no set-up to follow, no
   reading load. Meme pages grow fastest on exactly this.
4. **Templates and recurring characters** let an audience learn the format
   once and enjoy it forever (animation memes, Simon's Cat: one silent
   hand-drawn cat, 148 episodes).
5. **Handmade beats polished right now**: crude, MS-Paint-like animation is
   described as a "lo-fi rebellion against AI"; it reads as a person, not a
   brand.

## What is funny (and why A/B were not)

1. **Benign violation** (academic, McGraw & Warren): something is wrong, a
   norm is broken, a body is in trouble, **and** it is clearly harmless. Too
   benign → boring (that was us). Too violent → upsetting. Distance makes
   violations benign: cartoons, exaggeration, "it happened to a doodle".
2. **Incongruity + surprise**: build an expectation, break it. The
   **rule of three**: two beats set the pattern, the third breaks it and is
   the funniest.
3. **Recognition laughter** (observational comedy): name the thing everyone
   has lived and nobody says. It must be **specific** ("the scoop is always at
   the bottom of the tub"), not general ("the gym is hard").
4. **Heightening**: a sketch is premise → escalate → button. Each beat is
   stranger or bigger than the last; never repeat the same joke at the same
   size. End on a twist or a hard stop, not a moral.
5. **Timing**: the *beat*, a held pause on a face before or after the turn,
   is where the laugh happens. Fast for chaos, a dead hold for deadpan.
   "Cut every word that doesn't earn a laugh."
6. **Slapstick is language-free** (Mr. Bean sold to 100+ territories) and
   animation is its natural home: impossible, consequence-free harm.
7. **Absurd/surreal**: play the illogical completely straight (the legs
   resign; the skeleton keeps waiting). This is the dominant register of
   Gen Z/Alpha humour.
8. **Anti-humour / deadpan**: sometimes the punchline is the look to camera.

## The gym's shared pain (the premise bank)

From the meme collections, the situations that recur, i.e. that the audience
already finds funny, most specific first:

- the day after leg day: stairs, chairs, the toilet, the penguin walk
- "one more" from a trainer or spotter who is lying
- the spotter who says "all you" while lifting the whole bar
- someone on *your* machine, on their phone, with "two sets left"
- curls in the squat rack
- Monday is international chest day; the leg area is a desert
- losing count mid-set
- the gym mirror vs. every other mirror; the pump that vanishes
- the scoop buried at the bottom of the protein tub
- forgetting headphones (the session is over before it starts)
- the person who grunts over a tiny weight; plates left on the bar
- the older regular who quietly out-lifts everyone
- cardio: 30 seconds feels like an hour

## How a brand does this without being an ad

- **Entertain first; the product is not the punchline** (Duolingo 09, General
  Mills, The Onion). The Onion's rule: the brand takes itself less seriously,
  or it does not work.
- **The account is the brand.** The handle and the bio carry "WTX", the video
  carries the joke. A small signature in a corner, the way an animator signs,
  is native; a logo sting is not.
- **A recurring cast and a running easter egg** build recognition without a
  pitch (the plate with eyes hides in every scene).
- **Relevance is allowed when it is the joke itself** (losing count *is* a
  tracking joke), never as a tag line.

## Rules for batch C

1. **Comedy first: every reel is one joke**, 7–12 s, premise → heighten → button.
2. **No tag, no logo sting, no CTA, no feature.** A tiny hand-written signature only.
3. **The caption is a meme caption**: white box, black text, how people post
   ("El día después de pierna:"), ≤ 8 words, on frame 0.
4. **Crude hand-drawn look**: wobbly black marker, flat fills, line boil, snap
   poses and held frames (limited animation), not smooth "motion design".
5. **A recurring cast with big expressive faces.** The face does the acting;
   hold on it for the beat.
6. **Language-light**: slapstick and expressions carry it; speech is gibberish
   blips with a short bubble, so ES and EN are the same video.
7. **A violation that is clearly harmless**, heightened three times, ending on
   a twist, a look to camera, or a loop back to frame 0.
8. **Built to be sent**: each one is "this is so you" about a specific gym
   person or moment.
9. Still obeys the Hook Gate (10): motion and a sound in frame 0.
